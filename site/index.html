Kāhui

Discord, in case the world ends.

An open-source alternative to Discord where communities are hosted by their members, not by a company. When you join a Kāhui server, your device becomes part of that community's network — members collectively store the history and keep the server alive. There is no central database, no login server, and nothing to sign up for.

If this project, its website and its developers disappeared tomorrow, existing communities would keep running.

No account, and nothing to sign up for. Windows and macOS will warn that the download is unsigned — the project has no code-signing certificate, because buying one would put a company between you and the software. Checksums are published with every release.

The Linux AppImage carries its own copy of the system webview, which is most of its size. On Debian or Ubuntu the .deb is a tenth of that and uses the one you already have.

Running a server, or prefer a terminal? Every release also ships a single-binary command line client with no window at all — about 6 MB, and statically linked.

Two minutes

# the same thing, from the command line client
./kahui

> /create Aotearoa
invite: kahui1XEcg4xA41QNfJis19rYRdWU6SPqQuN1dGbfR1HAq3o…

# send that line to a friend. on their machine:
> /join kahui1XEcg4xA41QNfJis19rYRdWU6SPqQuN1dGbfR1HAq3o…
> kia ora

That invite is the only bootstrap step in the system. It is a hint about where to look, not a credential — everything it points at is verified against signatures once fetched.

How it works

  • A community is a log of signed events. Every message, channel and join is one event, signed with a key that never leaves your machine. Because each event proves its own authorship, your node can accept history from any peer without trusting that peer — which is what removes the server, rather than relocating it.
  • Catching up costs one round trip. Each member's events form a numbered chain, so "everything I have from Alice" is a single number. Send that, receive what it does not cover. It costs the same after five months offline as after five minutes.
  • Nobody is load-bearing, including the founder. Members tell each other where they are, so the network fills in to a mesh within seconds of you joining. Close the laptop that created the community and everyone else keeps talking.

Where it is up to

Milestone 2: a working desktop app. The protocol, storage, networking and sync are real, tested, and do everything described above, and there is now a window as well as a command line client. No voice, roles or moderation yet. Nodes behind strict home routers cannot reach each other across the internet yet — relay support is next. The architecture notes list every known limitation plainly, because a decentralised system that overstates its guarantees is worse than a centralised one that does not.
